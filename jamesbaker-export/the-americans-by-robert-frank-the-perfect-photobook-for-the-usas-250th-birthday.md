I bought <cite>The Americans</cite> by Robert Frank earlier this week, as part of finally getting some photobooks I’ve had my eyes on for a while. According to the back cover <cite>The Americans</cite> is <q>the most influential photobook of all time</q>, containing photos taken by Frank as he traveled post-war America. 

I decided to save going through the book until today – the USA’s 250th birthday. 

As a Brit, I’ve only been to the USA once: a week in New York City. As a permanent resident of York, I can tell you that the _new_ version doesn’t have much in common with the original. But overall, my experience of America comes from their media.

That leads nicely into what stood out most to me in my first look through <cite>The Americans</cite>: the variety. There are photos of Hollywood premieres, Detroit factories, coffee shops, political rallies and funerals. It’s a difficult task to capture a whole nation – especially one as big as the USA – but Robert Frank gave it a hell of a shot. Just the name <q><cite>The Americans</cite></q> conveys what he was trying to achieve.

All of these photos were taken well before even my parents were born. So I have no idea how an American that lived through these years would react to these images. To my eyes they’re an example of how art can be a time capsule. And a reminder to keep taking photographs of my own _everyday_. At some point this’ll be the past, and it’ll be nice to know how I _saw_ it.
